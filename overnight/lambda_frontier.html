What was actually asked

The open question was H1′: LISA regresses to the mean in the high band — but is the deficit big enough to be worth correcting on a properly trained model? Every previous run here answered from one of two useless corners. At λ = 1.0 the model matched magnitudes with random phase and scored −5.8 dB SNR, worse than emitting silence. At the official λ = 10⁻³ on 0.54 hours it learned the baseband perfectly and emitted nothing at all above 6 kHz.

Tonight's run puts λ on a dial and turns it, with everything else nailed down: same initialisation for the ReLU arms, same batches drawn from the same generator, same schedule, same 69×-larger corpus.

The frontier

Three arms, three panels: what the spectral term buys, what it costs, and where that lands against the paper's headline metric.

Three things fall out

H1′magnitude
The dial exists, and it is monotone Deficit tracks λ smoothly: −17.4, −4.5, −2.3 dB across two decades, with the baseband exact (−0.04 dB) and phase coherent throughout. The −2…−6 dB range inferred from the paper's own table last week is now observed. At the λ that produces paper-like LSD, headroom is a few dB — real, but nothing like the 20 dB the starved runs implied.
H2transport
Tested, and it largely fails relu_l1e-2 gave the ladder its first legitimate source, and the ladder did not survive contact with it. The best deterministic rung improves HB‑LSD by 1.7 %, not the 42 % it scores on a degenerate source. The difference was the energy‑inflation artefact that the mis‑specified‑map control had been flagging from the beginning.
H3sampling
Survives, and now carries the project The stochastic rung improves CRPS by 21.3 % on the coherent source — slightly more than on the degenerate one, so it is not living off a broken baseline. A proper scoring rule on a real source still rewards sampling over every deterministic map. That is where the generative claim actually lives.
H4metrics
Supported again, and sharpened λ=10⁻¹ has the best LSD (0.948) and the worst waveform fidelity (−1.50 dB vs naive). LSD ranks these four arms in exactly the reverse order of SNR. It cannot be the primary judge, which the notebook said before any of this ran.
—architecture
Suspect cleared The notebook's standing question — whether ReLU spectral bias is a second, independent cause of over‑smoothing — gets a clean negative. Same encoder, same latents, same loss, same batches; only the decoder's coordinate view changes to Fourier features. Deficit moves 0.6 dB on a 17 dB hole. It is the objective and the data, not the decoder. Strike it from the open list.

The mis‑specified‑map control fires correctly for the first time. On the degenerate source a deliberately wrong map improved HB‑LSD from 1.965 to 1.279 — the artefact that forced the standing warning about generic energy inflation. On the coherent source the same wrong map scores 1.167 against T0's 1.057: it now hurts, as it always should have. The control was right; the metric was being gamed; the fix was a better source, not a better metric.

And with the artefact removed, the deterministic ladder buys almost nothing. T1 moves HB‑LSD by 0.018 — 1.7 % — for 0.41 dB of waveform SNR. The 42 % win on the degenerate source was substantially the energy inflation its own control had been flagging all along.

T1 does still halve the energy deficit (−13.2 → −7.9 dB), and that is worth keeping: on an honest source the high‑band structure is already close to right, so closing the energy gap barely moves a log‑spectral distance.

CRPS is the exception, and it is the one that matters. The stochastic rung improves it by 21.3 % on the coherent source — slightly more than on the degenerate one, so this is not an artefact of a broken baseline. A proper scoring rule, on a real source, still rewards sampling over any deterministic map. That is exactly the claim §12 was built to make, and it is now the only rung standing.

The paper's ablation is scale‑dependent

LISA reports that deleting the spectral term entirely moves LSD by ≤0.01. Here, one decade of λ moves it by 0.58. Both can be true, and the reconciliation is the interesting part.

The spectral term is a crutch for a model that cannot learn the high band from data. At 17 epochs on 37 hours, the L1‑only model has no way to predict what lives above 6 kHz, so the loss‑optimal output is zero and it emits nothing. Turn the magnitude term up and it is forced to synthesise. A flat ablation is evidence that their model had already learned the high band conditionally.

Which means the ablation should never be quoted without its scale. It is a statement about a fully‑trained LISA, not about the loss in general.

What still doesn't reproduce

No arm beats naive polyphase upsampling. The best is 0.04 dB below it. The paper reports 24.16 dB, roughly 5 dB above where a sinc interpolator sits on this data. This reimplementation now reproduces LISA's LSD and still not its SNR.

One observation, recorded as a question rather than a claim: in the paper's own Table 1, AudioUNet (18.55), TFilm (19.51) and WSRGlow (19.41) all land within about a dB of naive upsampling on our data (19.20) — while LISA alone sits 5 dB clear of all three. A gap that size over three unrelated architectures, every one of them at the trivial baseline, is worth understanding before it is adopted as the target. Candidate explanations: a different SNR convention, a different downsampling operator (they specify sinc interpolation, we use resample_poly), or the input band entering their average differently.

Next, in order

1. Put the effort into the stochastic rung, not T1–T3. CRPS is the only score that moved on an honest source, and it moved by a fifth. The deterministic maps have had their test. 2. Finish the budget: 105k steps at the official λ to see whether an L1‑only model eventually learns the high band, which is the paper's implicit claim. About ten hours for one arm. 3. Settle the SNR convention against the official repository before treating 24.16 dB as a target. 4. Perceptual adjudication is now the binding constraint. No metric here separates "sharper" from "sounds better", and a 1.7 % HB‑LSD move certainly does not.
